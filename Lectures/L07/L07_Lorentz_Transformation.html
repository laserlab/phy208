Thursday’s exit ticket

“At \(0.87c\) a spaceship is measured at half its length, so the hull must be squeezed under enormous stress.”

No stress, no strain: every gauge on board reads zero, and the shorter number is still a correct measurement.

The word to watch is “it only looks shorter.” No. It is measured shorter — and today’s algebra says why.

Six lectures, one line

We have spent three weeks arguing in words and triangles.

The results are real. The method does not scale.

Today: the algebra that does all of it at once, derived, not asserted.

What the transformation has to do

Given an event at \((t, x)\) in my frame, return \((t', x')\) in the ship’s, where the ship moves at \(v\) along \(x\).

it must because
be linear uniform motion stays uniform motion in every inertial frame
send \(x = vt \to x' = 0\) the ship’s origin is at rest on the ship, by definition
send \(x = ct \to x' = ct'\) postulate 2: light is \(c\) for both of us
reduce to \(x' = x - vt\) when \(v \ll c\) Newton works, and we must reproduce him

Two lines of algebra

Linearity plus “the ship’s origin has \(x' = 0\)” forces the form

\[x' = \gamma\,(x - vt)\]

for some \(\gamma\) that may depend on \(v\) but not on \(x\) or \(t\).

By postulate 1, the ship’s view of me is the same law with \(v \to -v\):

\[x = \gamma\,(x' + vt')\]

The same \(\gamma\) — neither of us is the one “really moving”.

Now let light do the work

Both of us must measure that pulse at \(c\):   \(x = ct\)   and   \(x' = ct'\). Four steps:

1. Put \(x = ct\) into \(x' = \gamma(x - vt)\):   \(x' = \gamma(ct - vt) = \gamma t\,(c - v)\)

2. Put \(x' = ct'\) into \(x = \gamma(x' + vt')\):   \(x = \gamma(ct' + vt') = \gamma t'(c + v)\)

3. Multiply the two lines together:   \(x\,x' = \gamma^{2} t\,t'(c - v)(c + v) = \gamma^{2} t\,t'(c^{2} - v^{2})\)

4. But \(x\,x' = (ct)(ct') = c^{2}t\,t'\) — the clocks cancel:

\[\gamma^{2}\left(1 - \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^{2}/c^{2}}}\;}\]

The transformation, both directions

Ship’s, from mine

\[x' = \gamma\,(x - vt)\] \[t' = \gamma\left(t - \frac{vx}{c^{2}}\right)\]

Mine, from the ship’s

\[x = \gamma\,(x' + vt')\] \[t = \gamma\left(t' + \frac{vx'}{c^{2}}\right)\]

The \(-vx/c^{2}\) in the time equation is the relativity of simultaneity, sitting in the algebra where L04 spent a lecture putting it by hand.

The crank, turned three times

“One clock, two ticks.” “Endpoints marked at once.” “Simultaneous for me.” — what does each say in symbols?

set and you get which is
\(\Delta x = 0\) (one clock, two ticks) \(\Delta t' = \gamma\,\Delta t\) time dilation
\(\Delta t' = 0\) (endpoints at once, ship frame) \(\Delta x = \Delta x'/\gamma\) length contraction
\(\Delta t = 0\) (simultaneous for me) \(\Delta t' = -\gamma v\,\Delta x/c^{2}\) simultaneity offset

Three weeks of argument, three substitutions. The physics did not change, the bookkeeping did.

Row 2, read slowly: measured, not seen

To measure a moving ship you mark both ends at once — that is \(\Delta t = 0\), and a length needs it: mark them at different times and the ship has moved.

Feed \(\Delta t = 0\) into the time equation and the ship gets \(\Delta t' = -\gamma v\,\Delta x/c^{2} \neq 0\).

My “at once” is not the ship’s “at once.” The crew say I marked the two ends at different times, and a late mark on a moving ship lands short.

Neither frame is looking at anything: both used a lattice of synchronised clocks, and \(-v\Delta x/c^{2}\) is how much the two synchronisations differ.

Velocity addition, and the wall at \(c\)

An object moves at \(u'\) in the ship’s frame. In mine:

\[u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + u'v/c^{2}}\]

   0.5c + 0.5c  ->  0.800000c
   0.9c + 0.9c  ->  0.994475c
  0.99c + 0.99c  ->  0.999949c
  light + 0.5c  ->  1.000000c

Nothing you can do gets past \(c\), and light plus anything is still exactly \(c\). The wall is not a rule added on top — it falls out of the algebra.

Fizeau had the evidence in 1851

Light in moving water. Fresnel’s ether: the flow adds only \(v\,(1 - 1/n^{2})\). Fizeau measured it.

Galileo u+v  :   224,906,231.6 m/s
relativistic :   224,906,228.7 m/s
Fresnel 1818 :   224,906,228.7 m/s

The drag coefficient is velocity addition to first order — the evidence, 54 years early, filed under the wrong theory.

Where Newton went

Set \(c \to \infty\) and the transformation becomes \(x' = x - vt\), \(t' = t\) exactly. Newton is not wrong; he is the \(v/c \to 0\) corner of a larger law.

The matrix

Two words before the algebra.

A boost is what we just derived: a Lorentz transformation to a frame moving at constant \(v\), with no rotation. Not an acceleration — nothing speeds up, we relabel the same events in someone else’s coordinates.

A matrix is the transformation written as one object acting on a column of coordinates.

If you have taken MTH 309 the next few slides are vocabulary you own; if not, this is what that course is for.

If you have never multiplied two matrices

It is one rule, used four times: row across, column down, add the products.

\[\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} p \\ q \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ap + bq \\ cp + dq \end{pmatrix}\]

That is the whole rule. In two slides the letters become \(\gamma\) and \(-\gamma\beta\), the column becomes \((ct, x)\), and the answer is an equation you derived this morning.

For matrix times matrix, the answer’s row \(i\), column \(j\) entry is row \(i\) of the first, down column \(j\) of the second. Four entries, four such sums.

The boost, as a matrix

Use \(ct\) rather than \(t\), so both entries carry units of length:

\[\begin{pmatrix} ct' \\ x' \end{pmatrix} = \gamma\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\beta \\ -\beta & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} ct \\ x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta = \frac{v}{c}\]

Multiply out the top row — across and down, as promised:

\(ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x) = \gamma(ct - vx/c)\).

Divide by \(c\) — the time equation from twenty minutes ago.

Set it beside a rotation

Rotation in the \((x, y)\) plane

\[R = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \phantom{-}\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}\]

Preserves \(x^{2} + y^{2}\).

Boost in the \((ct, x)\) plane

\[\Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh\phi & -\sinh\phi \\ -\sinh\phi & \phantom{-}\cosh\phi \end{pmatrix}\]

Preserves something we name on Thursday.

\(\gamma = \cosh\phi\) and \(\gamma\beta = \sinh\phi\). Same structure — one sign, flipped.

Composing boosts is matrix multiplication

boost(0.6) then boost(0.7)  ->  combined beta = 0.915493
velocity-addition formula   ->  0.915493
Galileo would have said     ->  1.300000   (faster than light)

Two boosts multiply to one boost — the velocity-addition formula is matrix multiplication. Poincaré, 1905: they “doivent former un groupe”.

What the matrix makes obvious

in equations in matrices
four coupled relations to keep straight one object, four entries
“combine two boosts” — messy substitution multiply, then read off
“does it ever exceed \(c\)?” — case analysis closure: boost × boost = boost
inverse: flip \(v\), re-derive \(\Lambda(-\beta)\), and \(\Lambda(-\beta)\Lambda(\beta) = I\)

This is what a change of representation buys: no new physics, a different set of facts sitting in plain view. MTH 309 is the course where that becomes a habit; in L14 the same column picks up energy and momentum instead of \(t\) and \(x\), and that object is what a physicist calls a four-vector.

Your turn: get your week back

In your pairs — one line of algebra each. Every tool you need:

\[\Delta t' = \gamma\Bigl(\Delta t - \tfrac{v\,\Delta x}{c^{2}}\Bigr) \qquad \Delta x' = \gamma\,(\Delta x - v\,\Delta t)\] \[\Delta t = \gamma\Bigl(\Delta t' + \tfrac{v\,\Delta x'}{c^{2}}\Bigr) \qquad \Delta x = \gamma\,(\Delta x' + v\,\Delta t')\]

The recipe — same four moves for every job:

  1. Write your setup as one Δ that equals zero.
  2. Pick the equation with your target left and your zero right.
  3. Cross out the zero term. Read off the result.
  4. Check against your row’s number on the next slide — no match, hand up.

The three jobs

\[\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - \tfrac{v\Delta x}{c^{2}}) \ \ \Delta x' = \gamma(\Delta x - v\Delta t) \ \ \Delta t = \gamma(\Delta t' + \tfrac{v\Delta x'}{c^{2}}) \ \ \Delta x = \gamma(\Delta x' + v\Delta t')\]

your zero find if your line is right…

No match → hand up now.

Two events are simultaneous in my frame, separated by 600 m along the direction of the ship’s motion. The ship flies past at 0.6c.

On the ship, which event happens first, and does the answer depend on the ship’s speed?

(a) The event at larger x comes first, and a faster flyby widens the gap

(b) The event at smaller x comes first, and a faster flyby widens the gap

(c) They are still simultaneous, since zero transforms to zero

(d) The order depends on the speed: below some speed one comes first, above it the other

The answer, and the number

gamma at 0.6c        = 1.250
dt' = -g*v*dx/c^2    = -1.501 us
so the event at LARGER x is earlier on the ship, by 1.501 us
  at 0.3c: gap = 0.629 us   (grows, never flips)
  at 0.6c: gap = 1.501 us   (grows, never flips)
  at 0.9c: gap = 4.132 us   (grows, never flips)
L04's train, 300 m at 0.6c  ->  0.75 us   -- half the separation, half the offset

The sign is set by the direction of the ship’s motion, not its size. Faster only means further apart, never reversed.

Poincaré, June 1905 — the christening, and our \(\gamma\) as \(k = 1/\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2}}\):

One page on, he names \(t'\): “the variable \(t'\) may be called the local time” — bookkeeping, not time.

Set \(c = 1\) and compare his eq. (1) to our matrix — identical, symmetric in \(x \leftrightarrow t\).

Why Poincaré did not write a matrix

The notation was 47 years old when he wrote that page, and he had read it.

1850 · Sylvester names the matrix — an array whose job is to generate determinants
1858 · Cayley the array becomes an algebraic object: multiplication is composition
1884 · Poincaré cites it: “Les remarquables travaux de M. Sylvester sur les matrices…”
1905 · Poincaré writes the transformation as equations, its structure as a group
1908 · Minkowski writes it as matrices: \(x = Ax'\), \(f' = A^{-1}fA\)

He was not missing the tool. He reached for a different one — and “doit former un groupe” is the deeper statement.
